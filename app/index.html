    Reading headcodes, colours and the signal panel

    Reading a headcode

    Every train on the network carries a four-character headcode. The signaller sees it in the box on the panel, and it tells you what kind of train it is.

    1ClassWhat kind of train
    ZLetterArea or route; some letters mark specials
    23NumberPicks out the individual train

    The same headcode can be used by different trains in different parts of the country on the same day.

    The first digit: train class

    0Light locomotiveA locomotive running on its own, no carriages or wagons
    1Express passengerAlso used for some priority trains such as test trains and specials
    2Stopping passengerLocal and all-stations services
    3Priority specialAutumn railhead treatment trains, some test trains and parcels
    4Fast freightFreight allowed up to 75 mph, often container trains
    5Empty passenger trainGoing to or from a depot or siding, not carrying passengers
    6FreightFreight up to 60 mph, and many engineering trains
    7FreightFreight up to 45 mph
    8Slow freightFreight up to 35 mph
    9Special classSome high-speed and international services, and other special uses

    Letters worth spotting

    1ZCharter or specialOften a railtour, sometimes a steam special
    1QTest trainNetwork Rail's measurement trains, including the yellow New Measurement Train

    Letters are a convention, not a strict rule, and their use varies by region.

    The signal panels

    Choose York, Clapham Junction or Crewe at the top of the Panel tab. Each is drawn like a signaller's IECC screen, built from Network Rail's own berth data. Each line is a platform road, with the approach sections at each end. Trains step from section to section ("berths") as the signals clear, so they jump rather than glide.

    • White track is occupied by the train in that section.
    • Red signal: on, at danger. Green: off, showing a proceed aspect (the feed doesn't say which). Hollow: not learnt yet, which fills in over the first hour.
    • The headcode box is coloured by delay. Tap it for the train.

    Network and Route

    • Network is a wall map of the main lines. Each stretch between principal stations is lit by the average delay of the trains on it. Tap a stretch for its trains.
    • Route shows one line in both directions. Tap Change to pick a main line or build your own, or open any train and tap Make this my route.
    • On the Map, tap a train to see the stations it has reported at since it started, joined up in gold. Tap it again, or its headcode in the bar, for the full details. Clear map removes it.
    • Tap the Live pill for a list of the trains in view, most late first.

    Not just passenger trains

    The map and panel use Network Rail's own signalling and movement data, so they show everything that moves on the network: freight, light engines, empty trains, engineering and test trains, and charters. National Rail's data only covers passenger services. A small number of sensitive movements are never published.

    Where the data comes from

    • Signalling (TD): which section every train is in, and signal states. Network Rail.
    • Train movements (TRUST): times at each timing point and how late. Network Rail.
    • Timetable: origins, destinations and planned stops. Network Rail.
    • Station boards: National Rail, via the Rail Data Marketplace.
    • Map: © OpenStreetMap contributors. Stations: uk-railway-stations, ODbL.
